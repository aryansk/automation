In late July 2026, one announcement changed how the world's goods move.

On the twentieth, Yemen's Houthi movement declared a naval blockade against Saudi Arabia. By the fifth of August, it had claimed eight strikes on Saudi-linked tankers — the latest, the product tanker Wafa, near the Red Sea export port of Yanbu.

Here is the question we are going to unpack: can the Red Sea reopen?

The short answer is that the world's shippers are already voting with their routes — and the detour around Africa is winning.

Start with the geography.

The Red Sea is not one door. It is two. In the north, the Suez Canal connects the Mediterranean to the Red Sea. In the south, the Bab el-Mandeb Strait separates Yemen from Djibouti — a passage barely twenty miles wide.

Together, these two chokepoints carry roughly ten percent of world trade and around eight percent of global liquefied natural gas. When both are closed, there is no practical shortcut between Europe and Asia.

That is the definition we need: a chokepoint is where a narrow passage becomes the only affordable route — and where a small disruption becomes a global price.

And this disruption is not small.

Ships that used to transit Suez and Bab el-Mandeb now sail around the Cape of Good Hope. That route adds roughly eight thousand five hundred nautical miles on a round trip between Asia and Europe — about ten extra days.

Suez container traffic is running near sixty percent below 2023 levels. The canal authority is losing toll revenue it counted on. Container rates on Asia-Europe lanes are running far above pre-crisis levels, and tanker war-risk premiums have jumped — insurers are pricing the chance that the next strike finds its target.

That is why markets care. A chokepoint closure does not just delay ships. It raises the cost of every voyage, every contract, every shelf.

So what would reopen the Red Sea?

The honest answer is deterrence, not distance. The detour can be survived; the question is whether enough naval force, convoy protection and negotiated restraint can make the Bab el-Mandeb safe enough to use.

The United States and allies have struck Houthi targets before, and the EU's naval forces have run escorts for years. But the Houthis do not need to sink ships to win. They only need to keep enough uncertainty alive that insurers raise premiums and companies keep their vessels on the long route.

The signal to watch is not the next headline. It is the behaviour underneath it.

Watch whether transit counts through Bab el-Mandeb start climbing again. Watch whether war-risk premiums fall. Watch whether the major carriers — Maersk, MSC, Hapag-Lloyd — return their services to Suez, or keep them on the Cape.

If the detour becomes the permanent default, the Red Sea is not a temporary disruption. It is a new cost layer in the global economy — one that every importer, exporter and consumer eventually pays.

Two straits. One question. Can the Red Sea reopen?

The answer is not yet. And in 2026, the world is still paying the price of the detour while it waits.
