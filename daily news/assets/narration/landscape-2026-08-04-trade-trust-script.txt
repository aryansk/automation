In 2025, the world moved more goods and services than ever before — about thirty-five trillion dollars' worth, a jump of roughly seven and a half percent.

That is the surprise.

Trade is not shrinking. It is growing at record speed. Containers are fuller, ports are busier, and the value crossing borders is larger than any year in history.

So why does it feel like the trading system is falling apart?

Because volume and trust are moving in opposite directions. The same year that set the trade record also produced new tariffs, new export controls, and a quiet retreat from the rules that made global trade predictable in the first place.

That is the paradox we are going to unpack: more trade, less trust. How can both be true at the same time?

Start with the record itself.

The United Nations trade agency, UNCTAD, estimates that world trade in goods and services grew by about seven and a half percent in 2025, reaching roughly thirty-five trillion dollars. Manufacturing — especially electronics — did most of the heavy lifting. Energy and cars lagged behind.

But here is the second half of the sentence, the one that usually gets left out: the same agency warns that the outlook is increasingly fragile. Growth is expected to slow sharply in 2026. The World Trade Organization projects goods trade growth of under two percent.

So the record year is not a sign that all is well. It is the last surge before the weather changes.

That brings us to the rule that made this system work — a phrase worth knowing: most-favoured-nation treatment.

It sounds grand, but the idea is simple. When a country gives one trading partner its best tariff rate, it must give the same rate to everyone. No favourites. One price for all. That single promise is what kept the global system from collapsing into a web of private deals.

The WTO says the share of world trade actually conducted on those most-favoured-nation terms has fallen to about seventy-two percent and is still declining.

That number matters. It means roughly a quarter of global trade is now happening outside the old rulebook — under regional deals, special exemptions, tariffs aimed at specific countries, or no clear rule at all.

Now here is where the paradox sharpens.

If trust is falling, why is volume still rising?

The short answer: because the world is not decoupling. It is re-routing.

Think of the trade map as a highway system. The roads are not disappearing. They are being redrawn. Cars are taking different exits.

The clearest example is North America. For decades, China was the United States' largest trading partner. In 2026, Mexico overtook it. Mexico's exports to the United States now top those from China — about four hundred and seventy-five billion dollars versus roughly four hundred and twenty-seven billion.

That is not a small story. It is the largest reordering of American trade relationships in a generation. It is being driven partly by tariffs and partly by companies choosing partners they trust more than the cheapest option.

The same pattern shows up across the map. Vietnam is pulling in more manufacturing. India is becoming an alternative assembly base. Taiwan's chip industry is being asked to prove where its products go. Export controls on advanced semiconductors are rewriting which chips can cross which borders.

The old question was: where is it cheapest to build? The new question is: who is it safe to build with?

That shift has a price, and markets are starting to price it.

Companies now say they are willing to pay ten to twenty-five percent more for supply chains built on trust rather than on pure cost. That premium is not an abstraction. It shows up in inventories, in factory locations, in margins, and eventually in the prices consumers pay.

This is why the trade story is also a market story. When resilience becomes a budget line, it changes earnings, inflation and investment decisions. The cheapest supplier is no longer automatically the winner. The trusted supplier is.

So let us be precise about what is really happening, because the language gets muddy.

This is not decoupling. Total trade between major economies is not collapsing. The United States and China still trade enormous volumes with each other. Global flows keep setting records.

What is changing is the geometry. Goods are taking longer routes. Suppliers are multiplying. Inventories are being held higher, closer to home, in more countries. Governments are building factories they used to import from.

The World Bank, the IMF and the WTO all point the same direction: trade is fragmenting into blocs that trade more with friends and less with everyone else.

The practical consequence is a quieter, slower, more expensive world — one where the same goods move, but with more paperwork, more distance, and more risk priced in.

Here is the honest uncertainty, stated plainly. Nobody knows how far this re-routing goes.

It is possible that tariffs come down, negotiations succeed, and much of the old system survives. It is also possible that the blocs harden, that trust keeps falling, and that trade keeps growing only inside smaller, safer circles.

The signal to watch is not the headline number. It is the behaviour underneath it.

Watch whether companies keep building factories in new countries, or start reversing. Watch whether trade agreements multiply or stall. Watch whether the share of trade on most-favoured-nation terms keeps falling, or stabilises.

The map is the story now.

Not how much the world trades, but who trades with whom, along which routes, under which rules.

Trade can grow and trust can fall at the same time — and that is exactly what the record year is telling us. The volume is real. The trust is the part still being renegotiated.

That is the market question for the next phase of the global economy: not whether the world keeps trading, but whether it can learn to trust again while it does.
