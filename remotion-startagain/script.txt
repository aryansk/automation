A few days ago, I was in the middle of writing a script. One of those rare sessions where the words were actually coming, where I wasn't forcing anything, just following a thread that felt alive. And then, my phone rang.

Twenty minutes later, I sat back down, looked at the screen, and felt nothing. The thread was gone. I spent the next forty minutes rereading what I'd written, making tiny edits that didn't need to be made, waiting to feel something that never came back. I ended up scrapping the whole session and starting fresh the next day.

And the frustrating part wasn't the call. The call was twenty minutes. The frustrating part was the forty minutes of sitting there pretending to work while actually just waiting. The interruption cost me twenty minutes. What I did after cost me the rest of the day.

And this is measurable. Researchers at the University of California, Irvine, found that after an interruption, it takes an average of twenty-three minutes to fully return to the task. Twenty-three minutes just to climb back to where you already were.

That kept happening. Different interruptions, same result. Someone at the door, a notification I couldn't ignore, a thought that pulled me somewhere else for five minutes. And each time, coming back felt like arriving at a place I used to live. Familiar from the outside, completely inaccessible from where I was standing.

So I started paying attention to what was actually happening in those first few seconds after I sat back down. And what I noticed was that I was always trying to find my way back by looking at the work. Rereading, scrolling, trying to reconstruct the feeling from the evidence it left behind. The way you'd try to figure out what a song sounded like by reading the sheet music.

The feeling was never in the work. It was in me. In a specific state I was in at a specific moment. And that moment was already gone by the time I came back looking for it.

A psychologist named Sophie Leroy gave this a name. Attention residue. When you switch away from something, a part of your attention stays behind, still stuck on the thing you just left. You're back at your desk, but you're not all there.

The fix, when I finally found it, was simple. And it had nothing to do with coming back. It had everything to do with how I left. Before the interruption takes you, even if someone is standing in your doorway, you write one thing down. The specific thought that was live in your head at that exact second. The word that wasn't right yet. The argument you were about to test. Whatever your brain was actually touching, you put it somewhere outside your brain. One line. Then you go.

Writers figured this out a long time ago without turning it into advice. Stop in the middle of a sentence you already know how to finish, and coming back the next morning is almost easy. Stop at a clean resting point, and you're staring at a wall. The open loop pulls you forward. The closed one makes you start over every single time.

Interruptions work exactly the same way. You can leave cleanly or messily, and one line is the entire difference between the two.

The other piece is the two minutes after you sit back down. The interruption hasn't finished yet. Whatever pulled you away is still running somewhere underneath your thoughts. A conversation still replaying. Something unresolved still processing. Trying to work through that is like trying to read in a room where someone is still talking. You're physically present and mentally somewhere else entirely.

Two minutes. Not to check something else. Just to let it finish. Let whatever happened settle. Arrive back in the room you're actually sitting in. Then read the line, and start from the middle of wherever it points.

One interruption handled badly costs you twenty minutes of real work. Three in a day, and the whole thing is gone. Not the time. The state. The kind of focus where work feels like it's going somewhere rather than like you're pushing it uphill.

In fact, the American Psychological Association estimates that constantly switching between tasks can cost as much as forty percent of your productive time. Not because the work got harder, but because you keep paying, over and over, to start again.

You can sit at your desk for six hours and produce almost nothing, because each time you came back, you started the climb from the bottom. That's not a discipline problem. That's a leaving problem. And leaving problems have leaving solutions.
